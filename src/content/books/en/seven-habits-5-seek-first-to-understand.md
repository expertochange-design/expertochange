## The chapter in brief (1:30–9:30)

### We were never taught to listen

Covey opens with a simple observation. Communication is the most important skill in life. We spend most of our waking hours doing it: reading, writing, speaking and listening.

Now think about how much training you got in each one. Years of school went into reading and writing. Some of us had speech or presentation lessons. But listening? Almost nobody gets formal training in listening. And by listening, Covey means really understanding another person from their own frame of reference.

So we are using a skill all day long that we were never taught. No wonder so many conversations go wrong.

### Diagnose before you prescribe

To explain the problem, Covey tells a short story about an eye doctor. A patient comes in complaining that he cannot see well. The doctor takes off his own glasses, hands them over, and says: these have worked for me for years, they will work for you too.

Of course, the patient sees even worse. And the doctor gets annoyed that he is not trying hard enough.

It sounds silly. But we do this all the time. Someone tells us their problem and we hand them our glasses. We give the advice that worked for us, in our life, with our history.

The principle behind Habit 5 is one you already trust in other places: diagnose before you prescribe. You would not trust a doctor who wrote a prescription before examining you. You would not trust a salesperson who pushed a product before asking what you need. Covey points out that a good professional always seeks to understand the situation first. Habit 5 asks us to bring the same discipline to our relationships.

### Listening to reply

Here is the line from this chapter that most people remember. Covey writes: "Most people do not listen with the intent to understand; they listen with the intent to reply."

When we listen to reply, we are not really listening. We are waiting. Part of our mind is already loading our answer. We filter everything the other person says through our own experience, our own autobiography, as Covey puts it.

He describes a ladder of listening. At the bottom, we ignore the person. Next, we pretend to listen: yes, uh-huh, right. Then there is selective listening, where we only hear the parts that interest us. Then attentive listening, where we focus on the words. Most of us rarely go higher than that.

The top level is empathic listening. And that is something different.

### What empathic listening really is

Empathic listening is listening with the intent to understand. Not to agree. Not to fix. To understand.

It means getting inside the other person's frame of reference. You try to see the world the way they see it, and feel it the way they feel it.

Covey makes an important point here. Empathy is not sympathy. Sympathy is a form of agreement, a kind of judgement. Empathy does not mean you agree with the person. It means you understand them deeply, both emotionally and intellectually.

He also reminds us that words are a small part of communication. A lot of meaning comes through tone of voice and body language. So empathic listening uses your ears, but also your eyes and your heart. You listen for feeling and meaning, not just content.

### Psychological air

Why does this matter so much? Covey uses an image I love. Imagine all the air in the room you are in suddenly disappeared. What would you care about? Nothing except getting air. Not this video, not your plans for the weekend. Only air.

Once you can breathe again, air stops being a motivator. Satisfied needs do not motivate. Only unsatisfied needs do.

Then he says: "Next to physical survival, the greatest need of a human being is psychological survival." That means being understood, affirmed, validated and appreciated.

When you listen empathically to someone, you give them psychological air. And once that need is met, they can relax and focus on solving the problem. Until it is met, they will keep fighting to be heard, and nothing you say will really land.

### Our four autobiographical responses

Covey says that when we listen from our own autobiography, we tend to respond in four ways.

We evaluate: we agree or disagree.

We probe: we ask questions from our own point of view, often to steer the person.

We advise: we give counsel based on our own experience.

And we interpret: we try to figure people out, explaining their motives and behaviour based on our own motives and behaviour.

None of these are evil. Sometimes they are exactly what is needed. The problem is that we use them automatically, before we understand. We jump straight in.

Covey illustrates this with a conversation between a father and his teenage son, who says school is a waste of time. The father keeps evaluating, probing and advising, and the son shuts down. The conversation ends with neither of them understanding the other.

### The stages of empathic listening

So how do we get better? Covey describes a progression of skills. I will put them in my own words.

The first stage is simply repeating what the person said. This is the weakest form. Done on its own, it can feel mechanical and even insulting. But it does make you pay attention.

The second stage is rephrasing the content in your own words. Now you are thinking about what they mean, not just the words.

The third stage is reflecting the feeling. You notice the emotion underneath. You sound really frustrated about this.

The fourth stage combines the two: you rephrase the content and reflect the feeling together. So, you feel discouraged because you worked hard and nobody noticed.

Something powerful happens at this stage. The person feels understood, so they open up. They start thinking out loud. Often they find their own answer, because you gave them room to see their own problem clearly.

Covey is careful to say this is not a technique to manipulate people. If you use these skills without a sincere desire to understand, people will feel it. The skill has to come from character. And it takes time at first, but it saves a huge amount of time later, because you are no longer solving the wrong problem.

### Then seek to be understood

The second half of the habit matters too. Seek first to understand, then to be understood. Habit 5 is not about staying silent forever.

Covey says that seeking to be understood takes courage. Understanding takes consideration; being understood takes courage. And he borrows three old Greek ideas to describe how to present your view well.

Ethos is your personal credibility, the trust people have in your character and competence.

Pathos is the emotional side: showing that you understand how the other person feels.

Logos is the logic, the reasoning of your actual proposal.

The order matters. Character first, then relationship, then logic. Most of us go straight to logic. But when you present your ideas after you have truly understood the other person, and you show them that you understand, your ideas carry much more weight.

### This is in your Circle of Influence

One last idea. Remember Habit 1 and the Circle of Influence? You cannot control whether other people listen to you. But you can always choose to understand them first. That is completely in your hands. And every time you do it, your influence grows.

## Putting it into practice (9:30–14:00)

Let's make this practical. Here are five things you can do this week. Pick two or three and really commit to them.

### 1. Pick one relationship

Think of one relationship where communication is not going well right now. It might be a colleague, a team member, a client, a partner or a parent.

Write down how you think they see the situation. Not how you see it. How they see it. Then, next time you talk to them, listen to check whether you were right. You will probably find you were missing something important.

### 2. Catch your autobiographical responses

For one full day, watch yourself in conversations. Every time someone tells you something, notice your first reaction. Did you evaluate? Probe? Advise? Interpret?

Don't judge yourself. Just notice. You can even keep a quick tally in a note on your phone at the end of the day. Most people are surprised by how often they jump straight to advice.

### 3. Use the reflect-before-reply rule

In your next three important conversations, follow one simple rule. Before you give your own opinion, reflect back what you heard, and include the feeling.

For example: so you're worried the deadline is unrealistic, and you feel nobody asked for your input. Then pause. Let them correct you or say more. Only when they say something like, yes, exactly, do you share your view.

This works very well in one-to-one meetings with a manager, in client calls, and in code reviews or design discussions at work.

### 4. Before a meeting, prepare their case

Here is one from the professional world. Before your next difficult meeting or negotiation, write down the other side's position as clearly and fairly as you can. Imagine you had to argue it yourself.

If you are early in your career, try this before asking for a raise, a new project or more flexibility. What are your manager's concerns? What pressures are they under? When you start by naming those, the conversation changes.

### 5. Practise the three-step presentation

When you do need to be understood, use ethos, pathos and logos in that order.

Start by showing you understand their situation and concerns. Then explain your view. Then give your reasons. Try it in your next email or proposal: open with one or two sentences that show you understand the reader's needs, before you make your request.

### A small warning

When you start doing this, it can feel slow and a bit awkward. That's normal. And remember, it's not a trick. If you are only reflecting feelings so you can win the argument faster, people will feel it. The goal is real understanding. Be patient with yourself. Like every habit in this book, it gets easier with practice.

## Closing (16:30–17:30)

Let's bring it together.

First: most of us listen with the intent to reply. We hand people our glasses before we understand what they need.

Second: empathic listening means seeing the world through the other person's eyes, and reflecting both their words and their feelings. It gives them psychological air.

Third: once someone feels understood, they become open. That is the moment to share your view, with character, empathy and logic, in that order.

Here is a question to think about this week: who in your life is waiting to feel understood by you? And what would change if you listened to them, really listened, before you replied?
