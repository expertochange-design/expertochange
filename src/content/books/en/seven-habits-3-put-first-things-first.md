## Opening (0:00–1:30)

Let me start with a simple question. Think about the last week. How much of your time went to things that really mattered to you? And how much went to things that just felt loud at the moment?

If you are like most people, the honest answer is a little uncomfortable. We are busy. We are tired. But busy is not the same as moving forward.

This is part three of my series on Stephen Covey's book, *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*. Today we are looking at Habit 3: Put First Things First.

Here is where it sits. Habit 1 was about being proactive: you are the one who chooses. Habit 2 was about beginning with the end in mind: deciding what you want your life to stand for. Habit 3 is where those two become real. Covey calls it the practical fulfilment of the first two. Habit 2 is the mental creation, the blueprint. Habit 3 is the physical creation, the building. It is the habit of personal management.

In this episode I will walk you through the core ideas of the chapter, then give you some concrete steps to try this week, and then I will share how I have tried to live this habit myself.

## The chapter in brief (1:30–11:00)

### Leadership first, then management

Covey makes a clear distinction. Leadership asks: what are the right things to do? Management asks: how do I do them well? Habit 2 was about leadership of your own life. Habit 3 is about management.

And management, for Covey, comes down to one thing: discipline in carrying out what you have already decided matters. Not discipline for its own sake, but discipline in service of your values.

He points out that this takes something only humans have: independent will. The ability to make a decision and then act on it, even when you do not feel like it. Covey mentions an essay about the common denominator of success. Its conclusion, in my words, is that successful people form the habit of doing the things others do not like to do. They do not like those things either. But their sense of purpose is stronger than their dislike.

That is the heart of this habit. You let what matters lead, and you let your moods follow.

### Four generations of time management

Covey gives a short history of time management, in four stages.

The first generation is notes and checklists. You try to remember things and keep track of them.

The second is calendars and appointment books. You look ahead and schedule.

The third adds priorities and goals. You clarify values, rank tasks, and plan each day in detail. This is a big step forward, but Covey notices a problem. Planning every single day in tight detail can become rigid. It can push people aside, because people do not fit neatly into a schedule. And it can make you efficient at the wrong things.

So he proposes a fourth generation. The goal is not to manage time at all. It is to manage yourself. Instead of focusing on things and time, you focus on preserving and growing relationships and on getting meaningful results.

### The time management matrix

This is the idea most people remember from this chapter. Covey says every activity can be described by two qualities: how urgent it is, and how important it is.

Urgent means it demands attention now. The phone is ringing. Someone is waiting. Urgent things act on us.

Important means it contributes to your mission, your values, your high-priority goals. Important things usually need us to act on them.

Put those two qualities together, and you get four quadrants.

Quadrant One is urgent and important. Crises. Pressing problems. Deadlines. You have to handle these, and you always will have some.

Quadrant Two is important but not urgent. This is preparation and prevention. Building relationships. Planning. Learning. Exercise. Real rest. Spotting new opportunities. Nothing forces you to do these today, which is exactly why they get pushed aside.

Quadrant Three is urgent but not important. Many interruptions, some calls, some emails, some meetings. They feel important because they are urgent, and often because they matter to someone else's priorities, not yours.

Quadrant Four is neither urgent nor important. Busy work, trivia, time wasters, mindless escapes.

Here is Covey's key point. People who live in Quadrant One get worn down. Crisis after crisis. And the only relief they find is escaping to Quadrant Four. Other people spend much of their time in Quadrant Three, thinking they are in Quadrant One, reacting to whatever is urgent and assuming it must be important.

Effective people, he says, stay out of Quadrants Three and Four as much as they can, because those quadrants are not important. And they shrink Quadrant One over time, by spending more time in Quadrant Two.

That is the big insight. Quadrant Two is the heart of personal management. When you plan ahead, maintain things, invest in relationships, and build your skills, many crises simply never happen. Prevention beats firefighting.

### Where does the time come from?

You might be thinking: that sounds nice, but where do I find the time for Quadrant Two? I am already full.

Covey's answer is direct. The time usually comes from Quadrants Three and Four. You cannot ignore the urgent and important. But you can stop giving so much to things that are merely urgent or merely distracting.

And that means learning to say no.

This is not about being rude or unhelpful. Covey describes saying no to other things "pleasantly, smilingly, nonapologetically." The point is that you can only say no comfortably when there is a bigger yes burning inside you. When you know what your first things are, it becomes much easier to turn down the second and third things, even good ones. Sometimes the enemy of the best is the good.

### Becoming a Quadrant Two self-manager

Covey then describes what a good planning approach should do. In short, it should connect your daily actions to your deeper values. It should help you keep balance across the different parts of your life. It should focus on Quadrant Two. It should include people, not just tasks. It should be flexible, so it serves you rather than ruling you. And it should be easy to carry with you.

Then he lays out a simple weekly process with four steps.

First, identify your roles. You are not just a job title. You might be an employee, a team lead, a son or daughter, a partner, a friend, a learner, a member of your community. And there is always the role of taking care of yourself.

Second, choose goals. For each role, pick one or two important things you want to accomplish this week. Many of them should be Quadrant Two goals, and ideally they connect back to your personal mission from Habit 2.

Third, schedule. Look at the week ahead and put those goals into specific time slots before anything else fills them. Covey puts it memorably: "The key is not to prioritize what's on your schedule, but to schedule your priorities."

Fourth, adapt daily. Each morning, look at your plan, respond to what has come up, and adjust. You are not rigid. You are anchored.

Why plan by the week rather than by the day? Because a week is long enough to hold balance across your roles, and short enough to be concrete. A day is too small a window. You might not see your family or your health goal on a given Tuesday, but you can protect them across a week.

### People and things

Covey makes a point that I find very human. You can be efficient with things. You cannot be efficient with people. You can sort your inbox fast. But if you try to rush a difficult conversation with someone you love, or with a colleague who is struggling, it usually goes badly.

So the fourth generation approach leaves room for people. If your plan says one thing, and a person in front of you needs you, sometimes the principled choice is to set the plan aside. Your values decide, not your schedule.

### Delegation: increasing your effectiveness

The last big idea in the chapter is delegation. Covey sees it as one of the most powerful Quadrant Two activities, because it lets you get results through other people.

He describes two kinds.

The first is what he calls gofer delegation. Go for this, do that, tell me when it is done. You manage the methods. You watch every step. It does not scale, and it does not grow people.

The second is stewardship delegation. Here you focus on results, not methods. You give people the outcome and let them choose how to get there. Covey lists five things to make clear up front: the desired results, the guidelines or limits, the resources available, how accountability will work, and the consequences, good and bad.

He tells a well-known story about handing his young son responsibility for keeping the yard green and clean. In my own words: he explained the result he wanted, let his son own it, and agreed on how they would check in. It took some patience, but his son came to truly own the job.

The lesson is that trust is a powerful motivator. When you delegate results, people grow, and you are freed for more Quadrant Two work.

## Putting it into practice (11:00–15:30)

Let me give you five things you can actually do this week.

**One: audit a few days of your time.** For the next two or three days, jot down roughly how you spend your time in blocks of thirty minutes or an hour. Do not judge it yet. At the end, mark each block with a quadrant number. One, two, three, or four. Most people are surprised by how much lands in Three and Four. That is not a failure. It is information. It shows you where your Quadrant Two time can come from.

**Two: find your one big Quadrant Two activity.** Ask yourself this. What is one thing I am not doing now that, if I did it regularly, would make a real difference in my work? Then ask the same question about your personal life. For an early-career person, it might be learning a key skill properly, or building a relationship with a mentor. For a manager, it might be one-on-one time with your team, or fixing a recurring problem at its root. For your personal life, it might be sleep, exercise, or a weekly call with someone you love. Write both answers down.

**Three: do a weekly plan, Covey style.** Pick a time, maybe Sunday evening or Monday morning, and give it thirty minutes. Start by writing your roles. Keep it to around five to seven, so it stays manageable. Next to each role, write one or two goals for this week. Then open your calendar and block time for those goals first. Treat these blocks like appointments with an important person, because they are. Only then let the rest of the week fill in around them.

**Four: practise one clean no.** This week, find one request that belongs in Quadrant Three, something urgent to someone else but not important to your goals. Say no to it, kindly and without a long apology. You might say: "Thank you for thinking of me. I cannot take this on this week, because I have committed to something else." Or offer a smaller yes: "I cannot join the meeting, but I can send my comments in writing." Notice how it feels. It gets easier with practice.

**Five: delegate one result, not one task.** If you lead people, pick one piece of work and hand it over as a stewardship. Be clear on the result, the limits, the resources, how you will check in, and what success looks like. Then step back. If you do not manage anyone yet, you can still practise this. Ask your manager to give you results instead of steps. Or, in a shared project, agree on outcomes with a teammate rather than splitting tiny tasks.

A small bonus tip: protect a short daily check. Each morning, spend five minutes looking at your weekly plan. Ask: given what has happened, what are my first things today? Then adjust. This keeps the plan alive without making it a cage.

And please be patient with yourself. You will slip back into urgency. Everyone does. The aim is not perfection. The aim is to shift a little more of your life into Quadrant Two each week.

## Closing (18:00–19:30)

Let me bring this together with three ideas.

First, effectiveness lives in Quadrant Two. The important but not urgent work, planning, prevention, relationships, growth, is what changes your life and slowly reduces the crises.

Second, you make room for it by saying no. Calmly, kindly, without guilt. A strong yes inside you makes the no much easier.

Third, plan by the week. Start from your roles and goals, schedule your priorities first, adapt each day, and delegate results so others can grow with you.

Here is a question to sit with. If you looked at your calendar for next week, would it reflect what you say matters most to you? If not, what is the one block of time you could protect?
