## Opening (0:00–1:30)

Let me start with a simple question. If you keep living exactly the way you lived this past month, where will you be in ten years?

Not where you hope to be. Where will you actually be?

For a lot of us, that question is uncomfortable. We are busy and we work hard. But where is all that effort going?

That is what this episode is about. This is part two of my series on Stephen Covey's book, The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People. In the last episode we looked at Habit 1, Be Proactive. Habit 1 says: you are the one in charge of your responses. You can choose.

Habit 2 asks the obvious next question. Fine, you can choose. So what do you want to choose? Where are you going?

Covey calls Habit 2 the habit of personal leadership. In this episode I will walk you through the main ideas of the chapter, then give you some exercises you can do this week, and then I will share how I have tried to use it in my own life.

## The chapter in brief (1:30–9:45)

### Start with the destination

The name of the habit is the whole idea in a few words: begin with the end in mind. Covey puts it this way: "To begin with the end in mind means to start with a clear understanding of your destination."

That sounds simple. But think about how rarely we do it. We take a job because it was offered. We take on a project because someone asked. We fill our evenings with whatever is in front of us. Days turn into years, and the direction of our life is set mostly by accident.

Covey opens the chapter with a thought experiment. He asks you to imagine attending a funeral, and slowly realising that it is your own. Several people are going to speak about you: someone from your family, a close friend, someone from your work, and someone from a community you belong to.

Then he asks: what would you want each of them to say? What kind of person, parent, partner, colleague or friend would you want them to describe? What would you want them to remember you for?

This can feel heavy. But the point is not death. The point is to find what you deeply value. When you picture the end, the things that really matter to you become very clear, very fast. And usually they are not the things that fill most of our calendar.

So the end in mind is not only the end of your life. It can be the end of a project, a year, a meeting or a conversation. The habit is to know what success looks like before you start.

### All things are created twice

The core principle behind Habit 2 is this: all things are created twice. There is a mental creation first, and a physical creation second.

Covey uses the example of building a house. Before anyone pours concrete, someone draws a plan. You think about how many rooms you need, where the kitchen goes, how the light will come in. You create the house in your mind and on paper first. Then you build it. If you skip the first creation, you pay for it later, with expensive changes in the second.

The same is true for a business plan, a holiday, a presentation, or a career.

Here is the important part. Whether you are aware of it or not, there is always a first creation in your life. The question is who is doing it. If you do not create your own direction on purpose, other things will create it for you: other people's expectations, old habits, your upbringing, the pressure of the moment.

This is where Habit 1 and Habit 2 connect. Habit 1 says you are the creator. Habit 2 says: now do the first creation yourself.

### Leadership comes before management

Covey makes a clear distinction here between leadership and management. Leadership is the first creation. Management is the second.

Management is about doing things well. Leadership is about doing the right things. Covey puts it in one memorable line: "Management is efficiency in climbing the ladder of success; leadership determines whether the ladder is leaning against the right wall."

That image stays with me. You can climb very fast and very well, and still reach the top only to find the ladder was against the wrong wall.

He also tells a short story about a team cutting their way through a jungle. Everyone is working hard and efficiently. Then the leader climbs a tall tree, looks around, and shouts that they are in the wrong jungle. And the busy managers below shout back: be quiet, we are making progress.

Most of us are good at managing ourselves, with lists, apps and routines. What we often lack is that first step of leadership: climbing the tree and checking the direction.

### Rescripting yourself

Covey says that each of us lives by scripts. These are the patterns we picked up from family, culture, school and experience. Many of them are useful. Some of them do not fit who we want to be.

Because we have self-awareness, and because we have imagination and conscience, we can look at those scripts and write new ones. Covey calls this rescripting. You become your own first creator, instead of only acting out a script someone else wrote.

Imagination lets you picture a future that does not exist yet. Conscience lets you check that picture against principles, against what is right and true. Together they let you write a better direction for your life.

### The personal mission statement

The most practical tool in this chapter is the personal mission statement. Covey describes it as something like a personal constitution. It is a written statement of who you want to be, what you want to do, and the values and principles that your being and doing are based on.

A constitution is useful because it does not change every time the weather changes. It is a reference point. When you face a hard decision, you can come back to it and ask: does this fit who I said I want to be?

Covey makes two points about it that I find important.

First, it takes time. A good mission statement is not written in one evening. You write a draft, you live with it, you come back and change it over weeks or months. And he suggests that the process of writing it is as valuable as the final text, because the process makes you think deeply about what you care about.

Second, it is personal. There is no correct format. Some people write a short paragraph. Some write a list of values. Some write a poem. What matters is that it is truly yours, and that reading it moves you.

### What is at your centre?

Next, Covey asks a deep question: what is at the centre of your life?

Whatever sits at your centre becomes the source of four things for you. Your security, meaning your sense of worth. Your guidance, meaning your direction. Your wisdom, meaning how you see life. And your power, meaning your ability to act.

He then walks through a list of common centres. People can be centred on their spouse, their family, money, work, possessions, pleasure, a friend, even an enemy, a religious community, or themselves. Each of these is valuable in its place. The problem comes when one of them becomes the centre. Then your sense of worth rises and falls with it. If you are work-centred and you lose your job, you lose more than a job. If you are centred on another person, their mood becomes your mood.

Covey's answer is to place principles at the centre. Principles like fairness, honesty, integrity, service and growth do not change with circumstances. They do not depend on other people's behaviour. When principles are your centre, you can still love your family, enjoy your work and value money. But you see all of those through a steadier lens, and you make your choices from a more secure place.

### Roles and goals

Covey then makes the mission statement practical by connecting it to roles.

Each of us has several roles. You might be a professional, a daughter or son, a partner, a parent, a friend, a team member, a student, a member of a community. One common mistake is to give all your energy to one role and let the others fade. You may do very well at work and slowly lose touch with the people you love.

So he suggests writing your mission in terms of your important roles. For each role, ask what you want to be and do in it. Then set a few goals for each role. That way your mission statement does not stay up in the clouds. It turns into actions in real parts of your life.

### Visualisation and affirmation

Covey also talks about using your imagination on purpose. If you know you tend to react badly in a certain situation, you can picture yourself, in detail, handling it the way you want to. You rehearse it in your mind before it happens.

He suggests that good affirmations are personal, positive, in the present tense, visual and emotional. Something like: I respond with wisdom, patience and calm when my children misbehave. You picture it clearly, and you feel it. Over time that mental creation shapes the physical one.

### Beyond the individual

Finally, Covey points out that families and organisations can do the same thing. A family can write a shared mission statement. A team or a company can too. The key is that people need to be involved in creating it. A mission statement handed down from the top, without involvement, rarely creates real commitment.

## Putting it into practice (9:45–14:15)

Let us make this practical. Here are five things you can do this week. You do not need to do all of them at once. Pick one or two and start.

### 1. Do the four-speaker exercise, in writing

Set aside thirty minutes somewhere quiet. Picture the scene Covey describes. Then write down what you would honestly want four people to say: someone from your family, a close friend, a colleague or manager, and someone from a community you are part of.

Do not write what sounds impressive. Write what would truly make you feel your life was well lived. Then underline the words that repeat. Those repeated words are often your core values.

### 2. Write a rough first draft of your mission statement

Use what you just wrote. Try to answer three questions in a few sentences each. Who do I want to be? What do I want to contribute? What principles do I want to live by?

Keep it rough, in your own words. Call it version one and date it. You will change it, and that is the point.

If you are early in your career and this feels too big, start smaller. Write a mission for just the next year, or just for your role at work. That is still a first creation.

### 3. List your roles and set one goal for each

Write down your main roles. Try to keep it to about five to seven, so it stays manageable. For example: professional, partner, parent, friend, learner, and yourself as a person who needs rest and health.

For each role, write one meaningful goal for the next week. Small is fine. Call a friend you have not spoken to in a while. Prepare properly for one important meeting. Spend one evening fully with your family, phone in another room.

You may notice that some roles rarely get any goal at all. That is useful information.

### 4. Begin each day and each task with the end in mind

You do not need a big ceremony for this. Before an important meeting, take one minute and write: what would a good outcome look like when this meeting ends? Before you start a report, ask: who is it for, and what do I want them to understand or decide when they finish reading it?

A clear picture of the end saves a lot of wasted effort in the middle.

### 5. Check your centre

Here is a quick honest check. Think about the last time you felt really upset or anxious. What was it about? Money, someone's approval, work, a specific person?

Often our strongest emotional reactions show us what is currently at our centre. You do not need to judge yourself. Just notice. Then ask: what principle would I like to stand on instead, in that kind of situation?

### A note on keeping it alive

A mission statement only helps if you look at it. Read it at the start of each week while you plan, and change it when it no longer feels true.

## Closing (16:45–18:00)

Let me bring it together.

First, everything is created twice. If you do not create your direction on purpose, someone or something else will create it for you.

Second, leadership comes before management. Before you climb faster, check that your ladder is on the right wall.

Third, a personal mission statement, built on principles and connected to your roles, gives you a steady centre. It helps you decide what to say yes to, and what to say no to.

Here is a question to sit with this week. If the people who matter most to you described your life honestly, what would you want them to say, and does the way you spent this past week move you towards that?
