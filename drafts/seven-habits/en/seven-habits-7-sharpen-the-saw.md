## Opening (0:00–1:30)

Imagine you walk through a forest and you see someone sawing down a tree. They look exhausted. They have been at it for hours. You notice the saw is completely blunt, so you ask, "Why don't you stop for a few minutes and sharpen it?" And they answer, "I can't. I'm too busy sawing."

That is a small story Covey tells, and I think most of us have been that person. I know I have. We push harder and harder with a tool that keeps getting duller, and we call that being productive.

This is the last episode in my series on *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*. Today is Habit 7: Sharpen the Saw. In a way, this habit is different from the others. Habits 1, 2 and 3 are about mastering yourself. Habits 4, 5 and 6 are about working well with other people. Habit 7 wraps around all six. It is the habit that keeps the others alive.

In this episode I will walk you through what the chapter says, give you a simple plan you can start this week, share a little of how I have tried to live it, and then close the whole series.

## The chapter in brief (1:30–10:00)

### You are the saw

The main idea is simple. The saw in the story is you. Your body, your mind, your relationships, your sense of meaning. That is the only instrument you have for doing anything in life: your work, your family, your contribution.

So sharpening the saw means taking time, regularly, to renew yourself. Not as a reward when everything else is done, because everything else is never done. It is something you protect on purpose.

Covey connects this to an idea from earlier in the book: the balance between production and production capability. If you only focus on output, on getting things done, and never look after the thing that produces the output, eventually it breaks down. A goose that lays golden eggs still needs to be fed. You are the goose here. Sharpening the saw is how you look after it.

### The four dimensions of renewal

Covey describes our nature as having four dimensions, and he says renewal means exercising all four, regularly, and in balance.

**The physical dimension.** This is about caring for your body: eating well, getting enough rest, managing stress, and exercising. He talks about three parts of fitness: endurance, flexibility and strength. You don't need a gym or expensive equipment. A few hours a week of steady exercise at home is enough to make a real difference.

What I like here is that he links the physical to proactivity. Most of us don't exercise because it is never urgent. It is the classic important but not urgent activity, what he calls Quadrant II in Habit 3. Nobody calls you and says, "Your run is overdue." So it only happens if you choose it. And every time you choose it, you are also strengthening your ability to act on your values instead of your moods. That is Habit 1, in your body.

**The spiritual dimension.** For Covey, this is your core, your center, your commitment to your values. It is very private, and people renew it in different ways. Some through prayer or meditation. Some by reading literature or texts that inspire them. Some through time in nature, or with music, or simply in quiet reflection.

He connects this to Habit 2. If you have a personal mission statement, spending time with it each day, reading it, thinking about it, keeps you anchored. It reminds you what you are about before the day starts pulling you in a hundred directions. He calls this a daily private victory: winning the battle with yourself in the morning, before you go out and deal with everyone else.

**The mental dimension.** Covey points out that a lot of our mental growth happens through formal education when we are young. Then we leave school and many of us stop. We read less, we write less, we stop learning new things in a disciplined way. And a lot of our free time goes to screens that ask nothing of our minds.

So mental renewal means continuing to educate yourself. Reading good books, writing, organising your thoughts, planning. He mentions keeping a journal, writing letters that go deeper than the surface, and planning your week. Planning itself is a form of mental exercise, because it makes you begin with the end in mind and organise around what matters.

He recommends trying to read broadly, not only in your own field. A book a month, then a book every two weeks, then more. Reading the minds of great thinkers is one of the cheapest and fastest ways to grow.

**The social and emotional dimension.** This one is a little different. The other three need time set aside for them. The social and emotional dimension is renewed mostly in your daily interactions with other people. It is tied to the public victory, Habits 4, 5 and 6: thinking win-win, seeking first to understand, and creating synergy.

Covey says this dimension depends on a sense of inner security. And that security does not come from what others think of you or how they treat you. It comes from inside: from living with integrity, from knowing your values, and also from service. Helping others in a meaningful way, quietly, without needing credit, is one of the most powerful ways to renew yourself emotionally.

### Scripting others well

There is also a thoughtful idea in this chapter about how we see other people. We are all being shaped, a little, by how the people around us see us. Covey calls it a kind of social mirror. If people treat us as lazy or difficult, it is easy to start believing it.

So part of renewal, and part of being a good influence, is to see people for their potential, not only their current behaviour. When you believe in someone, and treat them as capable, you help them grow into that. That is something you can give to the people around you every day.

### Balance and synergy

Covey insists that the four dimensions need balance. If you neglect one, it affects the others. Someone who is physically fit but spiritually empty, or intellectually brilliant but cut off from people, is not truly effective.

And they reinforce each other. When you exercise, you also strengthen your will, which helps your spirit. When you renew your spirit, you treat people better. When you read and plan, you manage your time and energy better, which helps your body. Covey links them to the habits too: physical renewal supports Habit 1, spiritual renewal supports Habit 2, mental renewal supports Habit 3, and social and emotional renewal supports Habits 4, 5 and 6. So working on one dimension is never wasted. It lifts the whole system.

His practical suggestion is about an hour a day for the physical, spiritual and mental dimensions. One hour, out of twenty-four, to renew the instrument you use for the other twenty-three. When you put it that way, it is hard to argue with.

### The upward spiral

The last big idea is the upward spiral. Renewal is not about staying in the same place. It is about growth. Each time you learn something, commit to it, and then actually do it, you rise a little. Then you learn more, commit to more, do more, and rise again.

Covey stresses the role of conscience here. He sees conscience as something we can educate and listen to, a kind of inner sense of what is right. The more we act on it, the clearer it becomes. The more we ignore it, the quieter it gets. Learning alone is not enough. Commitment alone is not enough. It is the cycle of learning, committing and doing, again and again, that moves you upward.

### Inside-out, again

The book ends where it began: inside-out. Change starts with you, with your paradigms, your character, your choices. Covey talks about becoming a transition person: someone who stops a negative pattern that may have been passed down in a family or a workplace, and passes on something better instead.

That is a quiet, powerful idea. You don't need to change the world. You can decide that a certain pattern ends with you.

## Putting it into practice (10:00–14:30)

Let me give you a practical plan. I want this to be something you can actually do this week, even if you have a full job, studies, or a young family.

**Step one: do a quick check of your four dimensions.** Take a piece of paper and draw four boxes: body, mind, heart, spirit. In each one, give yourself a score from one to ten for how well you have renewed that area in the last month. Don't overthink it. Then circle the lowest score. That is your starting point. Most people find one dimension has been quietly ignored for a long time.

**Step two: choose one small action for each dimension.** Small enough that you can't really fail. For example:

- Body: a twenty-minute walk four days this week, or going to bed thirty minutes earlier.
- Mind: read fifteen pages a day of a book outside your usual field, or write one page in a journal each evening.
- Heart: have one real conversation this week where you only listen and try to understand, with no advice. Or do one helpful thing for someone without telling anyone.
- Spirit: spend ten minutes each morning in quiet, before you look at your phone. Read your values or mission statement, or just sit and think about what matters today.

**Step three: protect your renewal hour.** Look at your calendar right now and block the time. If an hour a day feels impossible, start with thirty minutes. Mornings work well for many people, because nothing has gone wrong yet. Treat this block like a meeting with someone important, because it is.

**Step four: plan your week with renewal in it.** Habit 3 taught us to plan weekly around our roles and goals. Covey's idea is to add sharpening the saw as its own role. So when you sit down each week to plan, the first thing you write is: what will I do this week for my body, my mind, my relationships and my spirit? Then you plan the rest around it.

**Step five: cut one drain.** Renewal is not only about adding things. Notice one habit that dulls the saw. Maybe it is scrolling late at night, or checking email the moment you wake up, or endless background television. Pick one, and reduce it this week. Use that freed time for renewal.

**Step six: close the loop on Sunday.** At the end of the week, spend five minutes looking back. What did I learn? What did I commit to? What did I actually do? This is the upward spiral in miniature. Learn, commit, do. Then set next week's small actions a bit higher.

If you are early in your career, I'd add one more thing. It is very tempting at the start to treat yourself as unlimited. Long hours, little sleep, no time to read or think, all to prove yourself. It can work for a while. But you will go much further over ten or twenty years if you build renewal into your life now, while the habits are still easy to form.

And if you are further along, managing people, the same applies to your team. Sharpening the saw is also something you can encourage in others: time to learn, to rest, to grow. A team that is never allowed to sharpen its saw will slowly get duller, however hard it works.

## Closing (17:00–18:30)

Let's bring it together.

First, you are the saw. Everything you do goes through you, so renewing yourself is not selfish or a luxury. It is the most important investment you can make.

Second, renewal has four dimensions: body, mind, heart and spirit. Tend to all four, in balance, and they will strengthen each other.

Third, small and steady wins. An hour a day, a plan each week, and the cycle of learn, commit, do. That is how you move up the spiral.

Here is a question to sit with: if your saw is the tool you will use for the rest of your life, what is one thing you will do this week to sharpen it?

Over eight episodes we went from the inside-out, from changing our paradigms, through private victory and public victory, to this habit of renewal that holds it all together. If you have followed along, thank you. My suggestion now is simple: don't try to master all seven at once. Go back, pick the one habit that spoke to you most, and live it for a month. Then pick the next. That is sharpening the saw too.
