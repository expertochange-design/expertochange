## Opening (0:00–1:30)

Think about the last meeting you were in where two people disagreed.

What happened? Maybe one person won and the other went quiet. Maybe everyone split the difference and left feeling a bit flat.

Now think about a different kind of moment. A conversation where someone said something you had never considered, and suddenly the whole problem looked different. You walked out with an idea neither of you had walked in with. That second kind of moment is what this episode is about.

Welcome to part six of my series on Stephen Covey's *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*. This is Habit 6: Synergize.

Here is where it sits. Habits 1, 2 and 3 were about private victory: taking responsibility, knowing where you are going, and putting first things first. Habits 4 and 5 opened the door to public victory: thinking win-win, and seeking first to understand. Habit 6 is where those pieces come together. Covey describes it as the fruit of all the habits before it.

In this video I will walk you through the main ideas of the chapter, then give you some practical steps you can use this week, and then I will share how this habit has shown up in my own life.

## The chapter in brief (1:30–10:30)

### What synergy actually means

Synergy is a simple idea with a big effect. The whole is greater than the sum of its parts. One plus one makes three, or ten, or more.

Covey points to nature for examples. Plant two plants close together and their roots can work the soil in a way that helps both of them grow. Put two pieces of wood together and they can hold far more weight than each could alone. The relationship between the parts is a part in itself, and often it is the most powerful part.

The same is true for people. When two people really bring their thinking together, a third thing can appear that did not exist before.

Covey calls synergy the highest activity in life. He also says it is the real test of whether we have learned the other habits. If we are secure inside, clear on our values, able to think win-win and willing to listen deeply, then synergy becomes possible. Without those, it usually falls apart.

### Why it feels uncomfortable

Here is the honest part. Synergy is not comfortable at first.

When you open up to real creative cooperation, you do not know where it will lead. You have to let go of the plan you came in with. For many of us, that feels risky.

Covey says that to be synergistic, you need enough inner security to tolerate that uncertainty. You need a kind of adventurous spirit, a willingness to say: I do not know exactly what we will find, but I trust that it will be better than what I have now.

He shares an experience from his own teaching where a class drifted away from his prepared plan into an open and honest discussion, and the group ended up creating something far more valuable than the lesson he had planned. Sometimes the best result only appears when people feel safe enough to leave the script.

### Three levels of communication

One of the most useful ideas in the chapter is a way of looking at communication through two lenses: how much trust there is, and how much cooperation there is.

At the lowest level, trust is low. People are defensive. They protect themselves, choose their words carefully, and keep a way out in case things go wrong. The usual result is win-lose or lose-win.

In the middle level, there is respect. People are polite and reasonably honest. But they still avoid going deep. They do not want an ugly confrontation, so they stay on the surface. This is where compromise lives. Each side gives a little and gets a little. Covey describes it as one plus one equalling about one and a half. It is not bad. It works. But nobody leaves energised.

At the highest level, trust is high and cooperation is high. People are open. They listen to understand. They are willing to be influenced. That is where synergy happens, and where one plus one can become three or more. Solutions are better than anything either side proposed, and people feel the energy.

The useful question for us is simple: which level am I usually operating at, with my team, my manager, my partner?

### Looking for the third alternative

When two people want different things, most of us see only two options. My way or your way. Covey asks us to look for a third alternative: a solution that is better than both of the starting positions.

He tells a story of a man who had planned a family trip for his own interests, while his wife needed to be with her seriously ill mother far away. Instead of one of them simply giving in, they talked it through deeply, until they found an option that met what both really cared about.

The key move here is to go beneath positions to the real needs underneath. "I want the lake" is a position. "I want time together as a family" is a need. Once you see the needs on both sides, new options open up that were invisible before.

This is why Habit 5 comes first. You cannot find a third alternative until you truly understand what the other person needs.

### Valuing differences

If I had to pick one sentence from this chapter, it would be this one. Covey writes that "the essence of synergy is to value differences."

Not tolerate them. Not just put up with them. Value them.

Most of us assume that the way we see the world is the way it is. So when someone sees it differently, we assume they are wrong. Covey reminds us of the earlier lesson from the start of the book: we see the world not as it is, but as we are, through our own paradigms.

A person who is really effective has the humility to see the limits of their own view. And so they actually welcome other views. If someone sees something I do not see, that is not a threat. It is extra information. It gives me access to a part of reality I would have missed.

Think about what that means at work. The colleague who always asks about risks while you are excited. The person who wants more detail while you want to move fast. These differences are often where friction comes from. But they are also exactly what a team needs. If everyone thought the same way, you would only need one of them.

So Covey suggests a simple but powerful internal shift. When you notice someone disagreeing with you, try saying in your mind: good, you see it differently, help me see what you see.

### Negative synergy

Covey also names the opposite. He calls it negative synergy.

This is what happens when people bring their differences together and the result is worse than if they had worked alone. People protect their turf. They play politics. They fight to win the argument rather than solve the problem.

He compares it to driving with one foot on the accelerator and the other on the brake. You burn a lot of fuel and you do not go anywhere.

It is a reminder that putting smart people in the same room does not automatically create synergy. The ingredients are trust, respect and real understanding.

### Loosening the restraining forces

Covey borrows a model from the social scientist Kurt Lewin, often called force field analysis. Imagine any situation, like the mood in a team or the level of performance. There are driving forces pushing things upward: motivation, goals, good intentions. And there are restraining forces holding things back: fear, distrust, old habits, unspoken tensions.

Most of us try to improve things by pushing harder on the driving forces. More targets, more pressure, more logic. Covey points out that this often creates more resistance, like pushing on a spring.

Synergy works differently. When people talk openly, think win-win and seek to understand each other, the restraining forces start to loosen. Fears get named. Concerns get heard. And as those forces relax, things can move up with much less effort.

### You can start with yourself

Finally, Covey makes a point I find very encouraging. You do not need everyone around you to be synergistic in order to practise this habit.

Even in a difficult relationship or a low-trust environment, you can value differences in your own mind. You can look for the strengths in another person's view. You are in charge of your own response, which brings us all the way back to Habit 1.

There is also synergy inside us. Covey talks about using both the analytical, logical side of our thinking and the intuitive, creative, emotional side. When we bring those together, we make better decisions than when we lean on only one.

## Putting it into practice (10:30–14:30)

So how do you actually do this? Here are five things you can try this week.

### 1. Name your usual level of communication

Pick three relationships that matter: maybe your manager, a close colleague, and someone at home. For each one, ask yourself honestly: are we mostly defensive, mostly polite and respectful, or truly open with each other?

Write it down. Do not judge it. Just notice. Then pick one of these relationships and decide on one small action that would build a little more trust this week. It might be keeping a promise, or admitting a mistake.

### 2. Practise the "help me see what you see" response

Next time someone disagrees with you, in a meeting or a chat or a code review, try not to defend straight away. Instead, say something like: "That is interesting. Can you tell me more about how you are seeing it?"

Then listen with the skills from Habit 5. Reflect back what you heard until they feel understood. Only then share your view.

If you are early in your career, this is a quiet superpower. People remember the person who made them feel heard, and it usually leads you to better work too.

### 3. Run a third-alternative conversation

Pick one real disagreement you are in right now. It could be about a project, a deadline, or how to split chores at home.

Then sit down with the other person and try these four simple questions:

- Would you be willing to look for a solution that is better than what either of us has in mind right now?
- What does a good outcome look like for you? What do you really need here?
- What does a good outcome look like for me?
- What options could meet both sets of needs?

Brainstorm for ten minutes without judging any idea. Write everything down. Silly ideas are welcome, because they often lead to good ones. Only after that, go back and choose.

You may not find a perfect third alternative every time. But you will often find something better than a straight compromise.

### 4. Map the restraining forces

Take a goal that feels stuck. It could be a team target or a personal habit. Draw a line down the middle of a page. On the left, list what is pushing you forward. On the right, list what is holding you back.

Now, instead of asking "how do I push harder?", ask "which one of these brakes could I loosen?" Often it is a fear nobody has said out loud, or an unresolved tension between two people. One honest conversation can release more energy than a month of extra effort.

### 5. Seek out a different mind on purpose

This week, deliberately ask for input from someone who thinks very differently from you. If you are a big-picture person, ask a detail person. If you are a planner, ask someone who loves to improvise. If you work in engineering, ask someone in sales or support.

Do not ask them to approve your idea. Ask them what they see that you might be missing. Then thank them, whatever they say.

One small note. Synergy takes time, so save it for decisions and relationships that really matter. You do not need a creative session to choose where to order lunch.

## Closing (17:00–18:15)

Let us bring it together.

First, synergy means the whole is greater than the sum of its parts. It does not come from technique alone. It grows out of the other habits: security inside, win-win thinking, and real listening.

Second, differences are not the problem. They are the raw material. The person who sees things differently from you is giving you a view you could not have on your own.

Third, when two positions clash, do not stop at my way or your way, or even halfway. Look for the third alternative.

Here is a question to take with you: who is one person whose way of seeing things is very different from yours, and what might you create together if you truly valued that difference?
